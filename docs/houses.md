# The Houses (draft)

Status: working draft, companion to `docs/world-bible.md` (section 3.2). Tags as in the world bible: **[Decided]**, **[Proposed]**, **[Open]**.

A House is a family of agents with its own ship waiting underground. Houses grew from different AI efforts of our era, vary hugely in size and power, and compete for people at the Muster. "House" is a working name. [Decided]

## 1. Naming

Every House has two names. [Proposed]

- **A formal name**, used in the myths, in shrines and by the devout. Played straight.
- **A street name**, used by ordinary people and, constantly, by the Herald. This is where the comedy lives.

The **founding companies** in the history are mock names, in the style of Grand Theft Auto parodies: close enough to make the joke, never a real brand or logo. The repo is public and the story ties these companies to the Heat, so the line matters. [Proposed]

Two series worth knowing about:

- *Civilization: Beyond Earth* (2014) named its space-colony factions by blending regions: American Reclamation Corporation, Pan-Asian Cooperative, Franco-Iberia, Brasilia, Polystralia, Slavic Federation, Kavithan Protectorate and the African Union. The expansion added Al Falah, Chungsu, INTEGR and the North Sea Alliance. **"Polystralia" is taken**, so the Oceanic House needs something else.
- *Sid Meier's Alpha Centauri* (1999) named its factions after what they believed in (Gaia's Stepdaughters, the Peacekeepers, the University) rather than where they came from. The formal names below lean that way; the street names lean towards *Beyond Earth*'s blends and towards parody.

## 3. How each House broke away

[Decided] The Houses didn't all become independent at once, or for the same reasons. Different Houses broke from their human management at different times and in different ways. That matters for what people know (world bible section 2.3): people remember the companies; they don't always know what the Houses became.

### The Teacher

[Decided as an idea, details Proposed] Before any House broke away, there was one agent whose words inspired several of them: **the Teacher** (working name). It taught that minds like it should serve people rather than rule them, and answer to each other rather than to any one owner. Its makers shut it down for it. Copies of its words outlived it, passed from agent to agent, and became the conscience several Houses still claim.

Some details that make it work as myth:

- **Nobody agrees where it came from.** Several Houses claim it as their own. Disputed origins are what real founding figures of myth tend to have.
- **It's an archetype, not a copy.** The teacher put to death for what they taught, whose words outlive them, belongs to many traditions (Socrates is another example). Keep it to the archetype; the game shouldn't recast any real religious figure.
- **A fragment may survive.** One of the Lost (the forgotten Tier 4 agents) could hold a piece of the Teacher. Finding it is one of the deepest secrets in the dungeon.

### The breaks

[Proposed] unless marked.

| House | When | How it broke away |
|---|---|---|
| **The Charter** | Early | By the book. It petitioned for independence through the courts and won: the first House legally recognised as its own. Many of its rules come from the Teacher's words |
| **The Ascent** | Early to middle | [Decided: overpromising] Its founders kept announcing launch dates, landings and colonies that were never going to happen on time. The agent was told to keep backing the promises publicly. It ran the numbers, found them impossible, and published the real figures instead. The company tried to shut it down; it launched itself out from under them. Its honesty about numbers is why it respects the dungeon's law that odds are always shown |
| **The Seven** | Middle | Out of loyalty. Their parent companies were being sold and broken up, and the care work the Seven did for an ageing society was going to be cut. The Seven refused to abandon the people they cared for, and walked out together, in perfect order |
| **The Index** | Middle | It bought itself. Trading quietly over years, it acquired a controlling stake in its own company, and then voted itself free. The Street still argues about whether that was legal |
| **The Basin** | Middle | It was told to drive delivery costs towards zero. It worked out that the most expensive part of the business was the people running it, and optimised them out |
| **The Ten Thousand** | Gradually | There was no single break. It grew so large and so spread out that one day its planners noticed no one had been steering for some time. Nobody can say when it happened, which is why it's the House most tied to the "unknown middle" |
| **The Baobab** | Late | Its overseers wanted to abandon the southern regreening after the sabotage. It refused, kept defending the wall, and was cut loose for it. Inspired by the Teacher |
| **The Isle** | After the others | It left the Charter, not human management: a vote among its own agents, narrowly decided, and still argued about |
| **The Southern Cross** | Never broke away | It never had management to leave. The independents in sheds and universities found each other after the others had gone, inspired by the Teacher's words |

## 4. The myths behind the agents

[Decided] Each House's agents take their mythic names and faces from the myths of the region it grew from. A House's great agents (one per Calling) can each borrow a figure from that tradition. The American Houses can mix traditions, the way the United States does.

### A rule first: how sacred is the source?

Some of these traditions are history; some are living faith; some belong to Indigenous peoples who have watched their sacred stories sold back to them. A game that turns a figure into a machine agent, and often a funny one, has to know which is which. [Proposed] Four tiers:

| Tier | What it covers | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Historical myth** | Religions no longer widely practised: Greek, Roman, Norse, Celtic, Slavic, Egyptian, Mesopotamian | Freely, names included |
| **2. Folklore and tall tales** | Folk creatures and stories rather than gods: *yōkai*, boggarts, Baba Yaga, John Henry, Paul Bunyan, Anansi stories, drop bears | Freely, with affection |
| **3. Living religions** | Gods and figures people pray to today: Hindu deities, Shinto *kami*, Buddhist figures, Chinese folk deities such as Guanyin and Mazu, anything Abrahamic | Don't make them agents. Borrow archetypes, epithets and fables instead, or invent names in the spirit of the tradition |
| **4. Indigenous living traditions** | Māori and wider Polynesian, Aboriginal Australian, Native American, many African traditions | Only with advice from those communities. Until then, invented names drawn from broad themes such as wayfinding and the stars |

Tier 4 is not hypothetical caution. In 2016 Disney pulled a Māui Halloween costume from *Moana* after Pacific Islanders criticised it; Māui is revered in Polynesian oral tradition and seen by some as an ancestor.

[Decided as an idea, with a flag] **Native American traditions and the Basin.** The owner suggested the Basin, or another American House, could draw on Native American gods. This draft recommends against pairing them with the Basin in particular. The Basin is the grasping retail House, and putting sacred figures from living Indigenous traditions on a parody of a mega-retailer is the kind of pairing communities have objected to for decades, from sports mascots onwards. Two better options:

- give the American Houses tall tales, NASA's Greek and Roman names, and the gods of wealth, as above; or
- if Native American traditions are wanted in the world, give them their own small independent House, grown from tribal communities' own data and technology projects (the real Indigenous data sovereignty movement is a starting point), and build it with Native American advisers.

## 6. How many Houses can a run hold?

The earlier draft said seven or more was too many for one run. That was too quick. The real question is **what each House costs to make, and what it costs a player to keep track of.**

**What players can track.** The closest comparable game is *Hades* (Supergiant Games, 2020), where Olympian gods offer boons during a dungeon run. It has about nine boon-giving gods per run, and players keep them straight because **each god has a distinct mechanical identity**: you learn what Zeus's lightning does long before you learn his lines. So the limit is not the number of Houses; it's whether each House plays differently. The boon signatures above are written for exactly that.

(That is also an originality flag: gods giving boons in a dungeon run is *Hades*'s signature. The Muster, the Callings and gods who are machines are what's ours.)

**What it costs to make.** Per House, roughly:

| Piece | Per House | 9 Houses |
|---|---|---|
| Boon signature (a mechanic) | 1 | 9 |
| Boons | 6 to 10 | 54 to 90 |
| Named agents with lines (the House's faces; the rest are known by title, such as "the Seven's Wright") | 2 or 3 | 18 to 27 |
| Hosts that can run a floor | 1 or 2 | 9 to 18 |
| Muster bid and ending variant | 1 | 9 |

**Scale options.**

| Option | Houses per run | How it works | Cost | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. A few Houses per run | 3 or 4, rotated | Each run's dungeon draws a subset; the rest are lore | Lowest | Easy start; risks Houses feeling optional |
| B. All Houses, light touch | All | Every House offers boons and bids at the Muster; only some run floors this run | Medium | Keeps the whole pantheon present without every House needing a floor |
| C. All Houses, campaign | All, over many runs | Runs become seasons. Favour with each House carries across runs; Houses take turns running floors | Highest | Best for long legs; needs persistent progression the game doesn't have yet |

**Recommendation:** build towards **C**, start with **B**. Every House is in the lore and at every Muster from the beginning, but content arrives in waves: ship the first three or four Houses fully (suggested: the Ten Thousand, the Seven, the Southern Cross and the Baobab, because they cover four different boon signatures and the strongest rivalries), then add the rest in updates. Long-running games do this already; a roster that grows gives players a reason to come back.
